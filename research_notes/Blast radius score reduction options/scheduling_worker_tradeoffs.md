# Scheduling: worker count, max load, simulated time and misses

Scope: how selector.py packs the selected tests onto 1-8 workers, how far that is from optimal, the worker-count tradeoff (2 s per worker against makespan), and alternative scheduling methods. All numbers below were computed on current main (08aeb05) selections, regenerated by running main's selector.py on each labelled set (BR_DATA pointed at the set, map.pkl/profile.pkl from the read-only checkout, BR_SRC = the pinned sqlglot checkout). The predicted totals matched the Docker-measured simulated seconds exactly (sample 97.076, holdout1 618.608, holdout2 545.397, holdout2u 534.169, mutants 1065.357, 0 misses on every set), so these really are the selections the board scores.

## What does schedule() do, and how far is it from optimal packing?

### Takeaway
schedule() is already optimal, or within milliseconds of it, on every labelled set. Exact branch-and-bound over k = 1..8 finds **0.000 s** to recover on sample, holdout1, holdout2 and holdout2u, and **0.004 s** on mutants. That is far below the board's 0.1 s rounding. Scheduling cannot deliver any part of a 74 s cut on any set.

### Cited Findings
- schedule() sorts the tests by duration, longest first (ties by test number). For each worker count w = 1..min(8, n) it does a greedy longest-processing-time (LPT) pass (each test goes to the currently least-loaded worker), keeps the cheapest max(load) + 2000 ms x w, and stops early once the lower bound max(longest test, ceil(total/w)) + 2000 w is no better than the best found so far. Empty bins are dropped. — [selector.py](https://github.com/Danixjg/blast-radius/blob/08aeb05/selector.py) lines 2710-2729; the README calls it "Longest-processing-time packing for each worker count from 1 to 8, keeping the cheapest max(load) + 2 s x workers" — [README.md](https://github.com/Danixjg/blast-radius/blob/08aeb05/README.md)
- The scorer counts cost in whole milliseconds: round(seconds x 1000) per test, max load + 2 s per non-empty worker. — [SCORING.md](https://github.com/Danixjg/blast-radius/blob/08aeb05/SCORING.md). selector.main() builds ms_by_num from /data/tests.json with the same int(round(s*1000)), so its predicted cost is the scored cost.
- Measured per set (own computation, research/scheduling/analyze.py). Columns are the sum of per-commit cost in seconds. "Exact" is branch-and-bound for every k, proven optimal on every commit within the time limit (0 unproven). "Trivial LB" is min over k of max(longest, ceil(total/k)) + 2k.

- Only 4 commits across all 322 are not exactly at the trivial lower bound. h33700 in holdout2/2u is 7.859 s against an LB of 7.856, and the exact optimum is also 7.859, so LPT is already optimal there. In mutants, m1x027 costs 11.580 s against an optimum of 11.579, and m2x004 costs 6.016 s against 6.013 (Karmarkar-Karp finds both). m2x026 is 6.426 s and already optimal (LB 6.415). — research/scheduling/analysis.json
- The catch-all schedule (all 1620 tests, 103.047 s total, longest test tests/test_parser.py::TestParser::test_max_nodes at 28.817 s) packs onto 4 workers for 36.817 s. That equals its lower bound max(28.817, ceil(103.047/4) = 25.762) + 8 = 36.817. k = 3 would cost max(28.817, 34.349) + 6 = 40.349, so 4 is optimal. — own computation from tests.json
- Literature context: LPT's worst-case ratio on m identical machines is 4/3 - 1/(3m) (Graham 1969) — [Wikipedia: LPT scheduling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longest-processing-time-first_scheduling); [Della Croce & Scatamacchia, "LPT revisited"](https://arxiv.org/abs/1801.05489) (a modified LPT improves this to 4/3 - 1/(3(m-1)) for m >= 3). Multifit (Coffman, Garey and Johnson 1978: binary search over the makespan with first-fit-decreasing) has a tight worst-case ratio of 13/11 — [Wikipedia: Multifit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multifit_algorithm); [Friesen, OSTI](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/5108217). Karmarkar-Karp largest differencing and Korf's Complete KK (CKK) are the exact and anytime methods for multiway number partitioning — [Wikipedia: Largest differencing method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Largest_differencing_method); [Korf, IJCAI 2009](https://www.ijcai.org/Proceedings/09/Papers/096.pdf); [Schreiber, Korf & Moffitt, "Optimal Multi-Way Number Partitioning", JACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/3184400?download=true)

### Inferences
- These worst-case ratios do not bite here. Instances are tiny (median 1-13 tests per commit) and dominated by one long test. Whenever the longest test is at least total/k, any list schedule that places that test alone is optimal, and LPT does exactly that.
- The only measurable scheduling gain is 4 ms on mutants (0.004 s), and it needs no more than a KK pass alongside LPT. It would not change the 0.1 s-rounded board score on any set. It is not worth the code or the risk.
- A worst-case guard is possible at no real cost: run LPT and KK for each k, then branch-and-bound only when the result is above the trivial LB. The exact search for all 322 commits finished in a few seconds of pure Python. For a future hidden commit with several mid-sized tests and no dominant one, this could save some milliseconds to tenths of a second. On present evidence the expected value is about 0.

### Gaps
- No labelled commit has a selection where LPT is far from optimal (for example many 1-5 s tests and no dominant test). How large LPT's gap could get on unseen hidden commits is therefore untested. It is bounded by the theory above but probably small in practice.

## Distribution of selections; where 2 s/worker dominates; fewer or more workers

### Takeaway
Worker count is already chosen optimally per commit. Worker overhead is 29-43% of simulated time: sample 42 s, holdout1 178 s, holdout2 166 s, holdout2u 160 s, mutants 304 s. Most of it is the unavoidable 2 s floor of any non-empty commit. No commit would gain from a different worker count. The levers that remain are emptying commits (saves at least 2 s each) and dropping the 3-5 very long tests that set the makespan. Both are selection decisions, not scheduling decisions.

### Cited Findings (own computation, research/scheduling/decomp.py, bottleneck.py, on main's stdout)
- Selected tests per commit (number of commits): sample 0:11, 1:12, 2-10:5, 11-100:2. holdout1 0:16, 1:19, 2-10:6, 11-100:9, 101-1000:8, >1000:8. holdout2 0:18, 1:16, 2-10:7, 11-100:10, 101-1000:4, >1000:10. holdout2u 0:16, 1:15, 2-10:7, 11-100:10, 101-1000:4, >1000:9. mutants 0:9, 1:26, 2-10:17, 11-100:29, 101-1000:12, >1000:7.
- Worker counts used (non-empty commits): sample k=1:17, k=2:2. holdout1 1:27, 2:14, 3:2, 4:7. holdout2 1:26, 2:13, 3:1, 4:7. holdout2u 1:25, 2:12, 3:1, 4:7. mutants 1:50, 2:28, 3:6, 4:7. No commit uses more than 4 workers. With the longest test at 28.817 s and the catalog total at 103 s, k >= 5 never pays.
- Cost split (seconds), simulated = worker overhead + makespan: sample 42 + 55.076. holdout1 178 + 440.608. holdout2 166 + 379.397. holdout2u 160 + 374.169. mutants 304 + 761.357.
- Commits whose makespan equals their single longest test (cost = longest + 2k, so packing is irrelevant): sample 14 of 19 non-empty, holdout1 36 of 50, holdout2 29 of 47, holdout2u 28 of 45, mutants 53 of 91.
- Gain from using more than 1 worker, compared with putting everything on one worker: sample 16.3 s, holdout1 636.4 s, holdout2 546.4 s, holdout2u 543.2 s, mutants 823.0 s. The multi-worker choice already captures this, and the k-sweep is optimal.
- The 5 longest catalog tests set most makespans: test_parser.py::TestParser::test_max_nodes 28.817 s, test_executor.py::TestExecutor::test_execute_tpcds 21.454 s, test_generator.py::TestGenerator::test_generator_memory 6.686 s, test_execute_tpch 3.863 s, test_optimized_tpch 3.537 s. Most frequent bottleneck test: holdout1 test_execute_tpcds (9 commits), then test_max_nodes (7); holdout2/2u test_generator_memory (9), then test_max_nodes (7); mutants test_execute_tpcds (16), then test_qualify_columns (11) and test_max_nodes (10).
- Catch-all commits (all 1620 tests, 36.817 s each): 7 in holdout1, holdout2, holdout2u and mutants each = 257.7 s per set. The oracle cost of those same commits is holdout1 189.7 s, holdout2/2u 111.4 s, mutants 65.5 s. Sample has none.
- Non-empty selections on commits with no failing test (each costs at least 2 s and has an oracle cost of 0): sample 1 commit / 2.466 s, holdout1 8 / 93.2 s, holdout2 9 / 101.8 s, holdout2u 9 / 101.8 s, mutants 21 / 335.3 s.
- Hindsight upper bounds on selection precision at the bottleneck. These need knowledge of which tests fail, so they are only bounds. Dropping the single longest selected test when it does not fail, then rescheduling optimally, saves: sample 0.395 s, holdout1 158.7 s, holdout2 140.8 s, holdout2u 140.8 s, mutants 335.1 s. Dropping every non-failing test of 3 s or more saves: sample 0.0, holdout1 188.1, holdout2 185.0, holdout2u 185.0, mutants 417.9 s. Biggest single cases: test_execute_tpcds on h12156 (18.6 s), h12158 (17.5 s), h33748 (17.2 s), m2x027 (18.6 s); test_max_nodes on m2x000 (24.6 s).
- Gap from current to oracle (failing tests only, packed optimally): sample 2.725 s, holdout1 308.8 s, holdout2 311.2 s, holdout2u 311.2 s, mutants 738.4 s.

### Inferences
- **Where a 74 s cut is realistic:** not on sample. Its whole simulated headroom to the oracle is 2.725 s, plus about 2 s of CPU, so roughly 4.7 s at most. On holdout1, holdout2, holdout2u and mutants, a 74 s cut is within the hindsight headroom (159-335 s from bottleneck tests alone). It would have to come from selection precision on test_execute_tpcds, test_max_nodes and test_generator_memory, or from shrinking catch-alls, not from scheduling. Scheduling contributes 0.000-0.004 s on every set.
- **A decision rule that couples scheduling and selection, not in the repo.** grep found no expected-cost or marginal-cost logic in selector.py. The schedule makes the marginal cost of a test t, Δ(t) = cost(S) - cost(S \ {t}), exactly computable in microseconds. Almost every test has Δ = 0, because it fits in slack under the bottleneck. Only the bottleneck test (or the last test on the critical worker) has Δ > 0, often 15-25 s. Under the 300 s miss penalty, dropping t lowers expected cost only if P(t fails | patch) < Δ(t) / 300 s. Break-even is about 6% for tpcds (Δ about 18 s), about 8% for max_nodes (Δ up to 24.6 s), and about 1.5% for generator_memory (Δ about 4.7 s). For Δ = 0 tests it is 0%: never drop them, since they ride free. Any precision work (profiling, dynamic checks, verification) should therefore target only the critical-path tests of each commit. Verifying, by running, only test_execute_tpcds / test_max_nodes / test_generator_memory when they are the bottleneck would cost their CPU time, about 21 / 29 / 7 s. That exceeds the expected saving unless their failure probability is low *and* they can be ruled out more cheaply than by running them. So the rule is only useful together with a cheap static or profile-based argument, not with running the test.
- **Fewer or more workers.** The sweep is exhaustive (k = 1..8 with a valid lower-bound cut-off; exact search confirms the chosen k is optimal on all 322 commits), so no commit gains from a different k. The 2 s per worker can only be avoided by sending an empty selection.

### Gaps
- Failure probabilities for the long tests per patch type were not estimated here. The break-even analysis above needs them to turn into a concrete gain. That belongs to the selection-precision researchers.

## Does duration noise matter, and does scheduling interact with misses or CPU?

### Takeaway
No noise: scoring uses tests.json seconds, which the selector reads, so the packing cost is exact. Scheduling cannot cause or prevent misses. Its CPU cost is about 45 ms for all 322 commits in total, about 0.01 s per set and below the board's resolution.

### Cited Findings
- A test's scored duration is round(seconds x 1000) from /data/tests.json; misses depend only on whether failing tests appear in any worker. — [SCORING.md](https://github.com/Danixjg/blast-radius/blob/08aeb05/SCORING.md)
- selector.main() reads durations from DATA/tests.json at runtime, not from a build-time copy, so a hidden set with different seconds is still packed exactly. — [selector.py](https://github.com/Danixjg/blast-radius/blob/08aeb05/selector.py) main(), line ~2795
- Measured CPU of schedule(), in-process, venv Python, process_time: 45.4 ms for all 322 commit schedules across the 5 sets; catch-all (1620 tests) 1.29 ms per call. — research/scheduling/decomp.py output
- schedule() is also called inside verify() as the benefit estimate (cost >= 2 x (0.35 s + recorded seconds)), so a better scheduler would change verification gating only when cost changes. Given the 0-4 ms gaps, it does not. — [selector.py](https://github.com/Danixjg/blast-radius/blob/08aeb05/selector.py) verify(), line ~2774
- Industry test sharding (pytest-xdist, Tuist and CI shard splitters) uses greedy LPT on recorded durations. pytest-xdist's default "load" mode does not order by duration, and "worksteal" exists because real durations are unknown in advance. — [pytest-xdist docs](https://pytest-xdist.readthedocs.io/en/stable/distribution.html); [Ned Batchelder, "Load-balanced xdist"](https://nedbatchelder.com/blog/202112/loadbalanced_xdist). In this challenge durations are known exactly, so the offline problem is fully deterministic and the dynamic/work-stealing techniques from industry do not apply.

### Inferences
- Since packing is exact and deterministic, there is no robust-scheduling or variance tradeoff to exploit. The only interaction with misses is the selection-side marginal-cost rule described above.
- Re-entry candidates on the scheduling side were checked: different heuristics (KK, multifit), exact search, a different worker cap, a different tie-break, and splitting tests (not allowed: a test is atomic). None yields more than 0.004 s on any set.

### Gaps
- Docker CPU was not measured, as instructed. The 45 ms figure is local in-process CPU, not container CPU.

## Literature: P||Cmax with per-machine cost, and test-suite parallelisation

### Takeaway
The problem is P||Cmax (identical machines, makespan) with a linear machine cost, solved by sweeping m = 1..8. For these instance sizes, LPT plus a trivial lower bound certifies optimality almost always. Exact methods (CKK, branch-and-bound, bin-completion) exist but buy nothing here.

### Cited Findings
- Identical-machines scheduling (P||Cmax) is NP-hard; LPT is a 4/3 - 1/(3m) approximation, and there are PTASs and exact methods. — [Wikipedia: Identical-machines scheduling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identical-machines_scheduling)
- Multifit's 13/11 tight bound; one source's proof history is contested, but the final bound is consistently reported as 13/11. — [Wikipedia: Multifit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multifit_algorithm); [Friesen 1984, OSTI](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/5108217); a Semantic Scholar abstract quoting "12/11" relates to a different algorithm variant and conflicts with these.
- CKK and later methods (recursive number partitioning, sequential/improved bin completion) are the state of the art for optimal multi-way partitioning. — [Korf, IJCAI 2009](https://www.ijcai.org/Proceedings/09/Papers/096.pdf); [Schreiber, Korf & Moffitt, JACM 2018](https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/3184400?download=true)
- CI test sharding in practice is duration-weighted greedy LPT packing. — [pytest-xdist distribution docs](https://pytest-xdist.readthedocs.io/en/stable/distribution.html); [xdist-scheduling-exclusive (long tests on dedicated workers)](https://github.com/andgineer/xdist-scheduling-exclusive)

### Inferences
- The per-machine fixed cost turns the problem into choosing m by minimising max(p_max, ceil(T/m)) + c·m (the lower bound). With c = 2 s, p_max at least 0.4 s and T at most 103 s, the optimum m is at most 4, which matches what was observed. For a non-dominated instance the continuous optimum is m* ≈ sqrt(T/c), which is 7.2 for the full catalog. p_max = 28.8 s caps it at 4.
- "Dedicated workers for long tests" (the xdist-scheduling-exclusive idea) is exactly what LPT already does whenever the long test is at least total/k.

### Gaps
- I found no published work on P||Cmax with an explicit per-machine fixed cost under that name. The closest framing is the sweep over m above, or "scheduling with machine cost", which I did not verify with a primary source.
